Constitutions and amendments

Who could vote in North Carolina?

The items below point to official or official-compilation texts. They are not a complete legal history, and they are not advice about any person’s eligibility today. Confirm current rules with the State Board of Elections and the official constitution.

How the written rules changed

  • 1776. North Carolina’s first constitution was adopted by the Fifth Provincial Congress and was not submitted to a popular vote, according to the N.C. General Assembly compilation of constitutions and amendments. Read a documentary edition of that text and the official compilation rather than a paraphrase of its freehold and taxpaying rules. Amendments to the constitution of North Carolina, 1776–1996 (NCGA Library) · 1776 constitution, Yale Avalon documentary edition
  • 1835 and later nineteenth-century revisions. Later conventions and amendments recast suffrage and eligibility. Official collected texts include the Constitution of 1868 (NCGA Library) and the General Assembly Library compilation of amendments, 1873–1967 (PDF), which includes the 1875 revision of Article VI and the later literacy, poll-tax, and “grandfather” provisions. Read those texts at the source; they are not restated at length here.
  • 1870, 1920, 1964, and 1971. The Fifteenth, Nineteenth, Twenty-fourth, and Twenty-sixth Amendments to the U.S. Constitution set federal rules on race, sex, poll taxes in federal elections, and the voting age of 18. National Archives, Amendments 11–27
  • Current Article VI. The official N.C. Constitution now states that only a U.S. citizen who is 18 years of age and who possesses the qualifications in that article may vote. It requires one year of state residence and 30 days in the precinct for state elections, and it disqualifies a person “adjudged guilty of a felony” unless “first restored to the rights of citizenship in the manner prescribed by law.” Article VI also still contains a literacy-registration sentence and photographic-identification provisions, including language that is contingent on the November 3, 2026 referendum described in the official text. North Carolina Constitution, official text

How constitutional amendments work

Article V of the U.S. Constitution describes how federal amendments are proposed and ratified. The president does not sign or veto an amendment. Article V, National Archives

For a current voter-registration or polling-place question, use the N.C. State Board of Elections and the polling place lookup.
